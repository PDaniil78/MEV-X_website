Where MEV Actually Comes From

Most atomic arbitrage on a chain isn't spread evenly across thousands of independent trades. MEV-X's attribution research on Polygon found the vast majority of it traces back to one place.

MEV-X Team Apr 9, 2026 6 min read
Answer first

Using four independent attribution methods across 1.05 million Polygon blocks, MEV-X's research traced 96.7% of atomic arbitrage back to a single source transaction, accounting for $334,799 in extracted MEV over the study period. Arbitrage isn't diffuse — it's concentrated at one identifiable point in the transaction flow, which is exactly the point MEV protection needs to target.

Why attribution matters

It's easy to think of MEV as something that happens diffusely — thousands of small arbitrage opportunities scattered across a chain's transaction volume, each requiring its own detection and response. If that were true, MEV protection would be a matter of covering as much surface area as possible. MEV-X's attribution research asked a more specific question: when an atomic arbitrage opportunity appears, where does it actually originate? Tracing that origin changes what an effective defense looks like.

The method

The study analyzed 1.05 million blocks of Polygon activity and applied four independent attribution methods to identify, for each instance of atomic arbitrage, the originating transaction that created the opportunity in the first place. Using multiple independent methods rather than one was deliberate — attribution claims are only credible when different analytical approaches converge on the same answer rather than each surfacing a different story.

The finding

They converged: 96.7% of atomic arbitrage traced back to a single source transaction, representing $334,799 in extracted MEV across the analyzed period. That's a strong concentration result. It means the overwhelming majority of arbitrage opportunity on the chain isn't the product of many independent, unrelated trades — it's created by one specific, identifiable kind of transaction, over and over.

What this means for MEV protection design

Concentration is good news for anyone trying to build a defense, because it turns a broad surveillance problem into a targeted one. If arbitrage opportunity were genuinely diffuse, internalizing it would require instrumenting every possible transaction type a protocol might see. Because it's concentrated, a system like Homelander can focus its capture logic on the specific execution path where the opportunity actually forms — the swap itself — and catch the overwhelming majority of the value without needing to chase a long tail of edge cases.

This is also the empirical basis for treating MEV internalization as tractable rather than aspirational: the research shows the problem has a concentrated, addressable structure, not an intractably distributed one.

  • 1.05M Polygon blocks analyzed, using four independent attribution methods.
  • 96.7% of atomic arbitrage traced to a single source transaction type.
  • $334,799 in extracted MEV accounted for across the study period.
  • Full methodology published as "The Origins of MEV: Systematic Attribution of Arbitrage Opportunity Creation at Scale" (arXiv preprint, 2026).
