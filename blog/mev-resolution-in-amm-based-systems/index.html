MEV Resolution in AMM-Based Systems: External and Internal Execution

A swap that shifts a pool off the market price doesn't leave things unresolved for long. Something closes that gap within a block or two — the only open question is whether it's an external party or the application itself.

MEV-X Team Dec 12, 2025 5 min read
Answer first

Post-swap price imbalances in an AMM get resolved one of two ways: externally, through open competition among searchers who pay most of the value away as priority fees, or internally, by the application itself capturing the imbalance atomically. Homelander is MEV-X's implementation of internal resolution, built to carry the same execution guarantees as the swap it resolves.

Resolution is not optional — only its owner is

Once a swap moves an AMM pool's price away from the reference price available elsewhere, the imbalance doesn't sit there. On any chain with liquid markets and active searchers, it gets arbitraged away almost immediately — usually within the same block or the next one. The gap will close. The only real variable is who closes it, and that determines who captures the value released in the process.

External execution: resolution through competition

The default path is external. A searcher's bot detects the imbalance, constructs a backrunning transaction, and competes — through a priority-fee auction, a private order-flow channel, or a builder relationship — for the right to execute it first. Whoever wins pays most of the captured value away just to secure that position, which means most of what gets extracted never returns to the pool that created the opportunity. From the protocol's perspective, this is pure leakage: value generated by its own trading activity, routed entirely to third parties.

Internal execution: the application resolves its own imbalance

The alternative is for the application that created the imbalance to resolve it itself, as part of the same transaction. This is what Homelander does: it executes the backrun atomically, inside the swap's own execution path, rather than exposing the opportunity to an external auction at all. There's no separate transaction to win, because there's no window in which the opportunity is visible to anyone outside the protocol.

Internal resolution only works if it carries the same guarantees as the trade it's attached to. If the capture step could fail independently of the swap, or execute in a way that touched the user's price, it would just be trading one form of risk for another. Homelander's capture logic is bound to the swap's own transaction: it succeeds or reverts together with the swap, and it never changes the price or outcome the user sees.

  • The arbitrage gap created by a swap gets closed regardless — the design question is only who closes it.
  • External resolution routes most of the captured value to validators and block builders as priority fees.
  • Internal resolution keeps the captured value with the protocol and its liquidity providers, as AMM yield.
  • Internal resolution has to be atomic to avoid introducing a new failure mode alongside the user's swap.

This distinction — external competition versus internal capture — is the design axis behind every product MEV-X builds. Homelander applies it to AMM swaps; the same logic extends to CEX–DEX spreads and lending liquidations, wherever a protocol's own activity is what creates the opportunity in the first place.
